## Overview

Multiple System Atrophy (MSA) is a rare, rapidly progressive, and fatal **sporadic neurodegenerative disorder** classified among the alpha-synucleinopathies. It is characterized by a combination of:

- **Autonomic failure** (orthostatic hypotension, urinary dysfunction, sexual dysfunction)
- **Parkinsonism** (bradykinesia, rigidity, postural instability)
- **Cerebellar ataxia** (gait and limb ataxia, dysarthria, oculomotor dysfunction)

MSA is distinct from Parkinson's disease (PD), though it can closely mimic PD in early stages — particularly the parkinsonian subtype (MSA-P). The hallmark neuropathological feature is the accumulation of **alpha-synuclein within oligodendrocytes** (glial cytoplasmic inclusions, or GCIs), in contrast to PD where alpha-synuclein aggregates in neurons (Lewy bodies).

There is currently **no disease-modifying treatment**. Management is entirely symptomatic and supportive. Median survival from symptom onset is approximately **6–10 years**.

---

## Epidemiology

- **Prevalence:** Approximately 3–5 per 100,000 population
- **Incidence:** ~0.6 per 100,000 per year
- **Age of onset:** Typically 50s–60s; onset before age 30 is exceedingly rare (and argues against diagnosis)
- **Sex:** Slight male predominance in MSA-P; roughly equal in MSA-C
- **Geography:** MSA-P predominates in Western countries (~70% of cases); MSA-C is more common in Japan and East Asia
- **Genetics:** Overwhelmingly sporadic; rare familial cases reported but no confirmed Mendelian inheritance pattern

## Pathophysiology

### Alpha-Synuclein as Central Culprit

MSA is defined neuropathologically by **glial cytoplasmic inclusions (GCIs)** — argyrophilic filamentous inclusions found predominantly in oligodendrocytes. These inclusions are:

- Primarily composed of phosphorylated **alpha-synuclein** (phosphorylated at Ser129) and ubiquitin
- Structurally distinct from the alpha-synuclein fibrils found in PD and Dementia with Lewy Bodies (DLB) — a difference that may explain distinct disease phenotypes
- Thought to form when oligodendrocytes take up alpha-synuclein produced by neurons (oligodendrocytes themselves produce little endogenous alpha-synuclein)

The protein **p25α** (TPPP/p25) plays a key role: it is normally sequestered in myelin but redistributes to the oligodendrocyte cell body in MSA, promoting alpha-synuclein aggregation and GCI formation.

### Key Molecular Players

| Protein/Marker | Role in MSA |
|---|---|
| Alpha-synuclein | Primary component of GCIs; misfolded and aggregated |
| p25α (TPPP) | Redistributes from myelin → soma; promotes aggregation |
| Ubiquitin | Co-localizes with GCIs; marks inclusions for degradation |
| Neurofilament light chain (NfL) | Elevated in CSF/blood; marker of neurodegeneration severity |

### Neuroinflammation

- Significant increases in **HLA-DR+ microglia** in the putamen and substantia nigra
- Elevated T-cell infiltration in affected brain regions
- Neuroinflammation likely amplifies oligodendroglial dysfunction and neuronal death

### Affected Brain Regions

Neurodegeneration in MSA is widespread and multisystem:

| Region | Clinical Consequence |
|---|---|
| Putamen and striatum | Parkinsonism (MSA-P) |
| Substantia nigra | Parkinsonism, dopaminergic deficit |
| Cerebellum and cerebellar peduncles | Ataxia (MSA-C) |
| Pons, inferior olives | Cerebellar features; "hot cross bun" sign on MRI |
| Intermediolateral cell columns (spinal cord) | Autonomic failure |
| Onuf's nucleus (sacral cord) | Urinary/sphincter dysfunction |

## Subtypes

MSA is classified into two clinical subtypes based on the **predominant motor syndrome** at presentation. Both subtypes include autonomic dysfunction.

### MSA-P (Parkinsonian Subtype)

- **Predominant feature:** Parkinsonism — bradykinesia, rigidity, postural instability
- Tremor is less common and prominent than in PD; when present, may be irregular or jerky rather than the classic pill-rolling tremor of PD
- **~70% of MSA cases** in the US and Europe
- Most likely to be initially misdiagnosed as PD
- Responds partially to levodopa early, but response is transient and incomplete

### MSA-C (Cerebellar Subtype)

- **Predominant feature:** Cerebellar ataxia — gait ataxia, limb ataxia, cerebellar dysarthria, oculomotor dysfunction (nystagmus, saccadic pursuit, gaze-evoked nystagmus)
- **~30% of MSA cases** in Western countries; more common in Japan/East Asia
- Previously called olivopontocerebellar atrophy (OPCA)

### Overlap

Both subtypes **always include autonomic dysfunction** — this is not optional. Autonomic failure is a core criterion for diagnosis. As disease progresses, the distinction between subtypes can blur.

## Clinical Features

### Autonomic Features (Present in Both Subtypes)

Autonomic failure is the hallmark that helps distinguish MSA from PD. Features include:

**Cardiovascular:**
- **Neurogenic orthostatic hypotension (nOH):** Drop in SBP ≥20 mmHg or DBP ≥10 mmHg within 3 minutes of standing; often symptomatic (dizziness, syncope, "coat hanger" headache)
- Supine hypertension (common, complicates treatment)

**Urogenital:**
- Urinary urgency, frequency, urge incontinence
- Incomplete bladder emptying / urinary retention
- Erectile dysfunction (often an early feature in men — can precede motor symptoms)
- Loss of libido in women

**Gastrointestinal:**
- Constipation (severe, often requiring pharmacological management)
- Gastroparesis, early satiety

**Thermoregulatory:**
- Anhidrosis (decreased or absent sweating) or hyperhidrosis

**Other:**
- Dry mouth, dry eyes
- Pupillary abnormalities

### Motor Features

**Parkinsonism (in MSA-P):**
- Bradykinesia
- Rigidity (cogwheel or lead-pipe)
- Postural instability and falls (often early, distinguishing from PD where falls are typically later)
- Tremor (less prominent than PD)
- Camptocormia (severe forward truncal flexion)
- Disproportionate anterocollis (neck flexion)

**Cerebellar (in MSA-C):**
- Gait ataxia (wide-based, unsteady, falls)
- Limb ataxia (intention tremor, dysmetria)
- Cerebellar dysarthria (scanning, slurred speech)
- Nystagmus and saccadic pursuit eye movements

### Non-Motor Features

- **REM Sleep Behavior Disorder (RBD):** Dream enactment behaviors; may precede motor symptoms by years. Important prodromal marker.
- **Sleep-disordered breathing:** Inspiratory stridor (a "strangled" high-pitched noise during sleep) — distinctive feature of MSA and a poor prognostic sign
- **Cognitive dysfunction:** Mild in ~40% of patients; does not typically progress to frank dementia (dementia within 3 years of motor onset argues against MSA)
- **Depression and anxiety:** Present in ~28–40% of patients; may include panic attacks
- **Dysarthria and dysphagia:** Progressive; aspiration risk increases with disease progression
- **Drooling (sialorrhea)**
- **Pisa syndrome / Camptocormia:** Postural deformities

### Red Flags Suggesting MSA over PD

| Feature | MSA | Parkinson's Disease |
|---|---|---|
| Autonomic failure (severity) | Severe, early | Mild-moderate, later |
| Urinary dysfunction | Common, early | Later, milder |
| Orthostatic hypotension | Prominent, often symptomatic | Present but usually milder |
| Levodopa response | Partial, transient | Robust, sustained |
| REM sleep behavior disorder | Common | Common (also PD) |
| Inspiratory stridor | Characteristic | Absent |
| Cerebellar ataxia | Present in MSA-C | Absent |
| Falls (early) | Common | Later in disease |
| Camptocormia/anterocollis | More common | Less common |
| Cognitive decline/dementia | Mild, late | Can be significant |
| Cardiac MIBG scan | Normal | Reduced (denervation) |

## Diagnosis

MSA diagnosis is **clinical**, supported by neuroimaging and ancillary testing. Definitive diagnosis requires neuropathological confirmation at autopsy. No single clinical test is diagnostic.

### 2022 Movement Disorder Society (MDS) Diagnostic Criteria

The **2022 MDS criteria** (Wenning et al., Movement Disorders, 2022) replaced the prior 2008 Second Consensus Criteria and introduced four levels of diagnostic certainty:

#### 1. Neuropathologically Established MSA
- Autopsy confirmation of abundant GCIs in striato-nigral or olivoponto-cerebellar systems
- Gold standard; the only definitive diagnosis

#### 2. Clinically Established MSA (High Specificity)
- Requires: Autonomic failure PLUS motor features (parkinsonism OR cerebellar ataxia)
- **PLUS** at least one MRI marker:
  - Putaminal atrophy, putaminal hypointensity with hyperintense rim (T2)
  - Cerebellar/brainstem atrophy, "hot cross bun" sign on T2
- **PLUS** levodopa-insufficient response (for MSA-P)
- Sensitivity ~16%, Specificity ~99% (high confidence when met)

#### 3. Clinically Probable MSA (Balanced)
- Autonomic failure PLUS parkinsonism OR cerebellar ataxia
- PLUS at least one supportive feature
- Sensitivity ~64%, Specificity ~74%

#### 4. Possible Prodromal MSA (Research Category — New in 2022)
- Nonmotor features (RBD, autonomic dysfunction) with subtle motor signs
- Not yet meeting criteria for established or probable MSA
- Designed to capture early/preclinical stages for research enrollment

**Important exclusions:**
- Symptom onset before age 30
- Family history consistent with known genetics of atypical parkinsonism
- Dementia within 3 years of motor onset
- Hallucinations (not drug-induced) early in disease
- Parkinsonian features responding well and durably to levodopa

### Diagnostic Workup

#### History
- Time course and rate of progression (MSA is rapidly progressive)
- Presence and timing of autonomic symptoms (often precede motor symptoms)
- REM sleep behavior disorder history
- Medication history (rule out drug-induced parkinsonism)
- Family history

#### Examination
- Orthostatic vital signs (lying, 1 min, 3 min standing)
- Neurological exam: tone, speed, coordination, reflexes, cranial nerves
- Speech and swallowing assessment
- Urological exam if applicable

#### Neuroimaging

**MRI Brain (essential):**
- **"Hot cross bun" sign:** T2 hyperintensity in the pons in the shape of a cross — caused by selective loss of transverse pontocerebellar fibers while corticospinal tracts are preserved. Highly specific for MSA-C.
- **Putaminal hyperintense rim:** T2 hyperintense rim at the lateral border of the putamen — associated with MSA-P
- Putaminal and caudate atrophy (MSA-P)
- Cerebellar, pontine, and middle cerebellar peduncle atrophy (MSA-C)
- Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI): Reduced diffusivity in putamen/cerebellum in MSA

#### Autonomic Testing
- Tilt-table testing (quantifies orthostatic hypotension)
- Thermoregulatory sweat test (TST) and quantitative sudomotor axon reflex test (QSART)
- Urodynamic studies (detrusor hyperreflexia early → underactivity later)

#### Cardiac MIBG Scintigraphy (MIBG Scan)
- [¹²³I]-MIBG imaging of cardiac sympathetic innervation
- **In PD:** Reduced/absent cardiac MIBG uptake (postganglionic sympathetic denervation)
- **In MSA:** Normal or near-normal MIBG uptake (preganglionic lesion)
- Useful for differentiating MSA from PD — especially in ambiguous cases

#### Other Studies
- **Polysomnography:** Confirm RBD, detect stridor, identify sleep apnea
- **Sphincter EMG:** Denervation of external urethral/anal sphincter (Onuf's nucleus loss) — supportive of MSA, though not specific
- **Neuropsychological testing:** Rule out dementia (DLB) if cognitive concerns

#### Emerging Biomarkers
- **RT-QuIC (Real-Time Quaking-Induced Conversion):** Detects MSA-specific alpha-synuclein seed amplification in CSF — distinguishes MSA-type from PD-type aggregates
- **Skin biopsy:** Detection of phosphorylated alpha-synuclein deposits in skin nerve fibers (experimental)
- **Serum/CSF Neurofilament Light Chain (NfL):** Elevated and correlates with disease progression; not specific to MSA but useful for tracking
- **PET imaging:** [¹⁸F]ACI-12589 — novel PET ligand for in vivo visualization of alpha-synuclein pathology (investigational)
- **Laryngoscopy:** Abnormal laryngeal cartilage movement in ~93% of MSA patients vs. ~2% of PD patients — promising early biomarker

## Differentiating MSA from Parkinson's Disease

The distinction is clinically critical because prognosis, caregiver planning, and treatment response differ significantly.

### Key Differentiating Features

| Feature | MSA | Parkinson's Disease |
|---|---|---|
| **Levodopa response** | Partial, short-lived (some initial response in MSA-P possible) | Robust, sustained |
| **Autonomic failure** | Severe, early, prominent | Milder, later |
| **Cerebellar signs** | Present (MSA-C) | Absent |
| **Falls** | Early (often within 3 years) | Late |
| **Dementia** | Absent or mild | Can develop (PDD) |
| **Visual hallucinations** | Rare | More common (especially with dopaminergics) |
| **MRI** | Structural changes (hot cross bun, putaminal rim) | Usually normal |
| **MIBG cardiac scan** | Normal (preganglionic lesion) | Reduced (postganglionic) |
| **Putamen volume** | Reduced | Normal |
| **Putamen glucose metabolism (FDG-PET)** | Reduced | Normal or mildly reduced |
| **Striatal D2 receptor density** | Reduced | Normal |
| **Inspiratory stridor** | Present | Absent |
| **Progression rate** | Rapid (wheelchair ~5–6 years) | Slower (decades possible) |
| **Response to speech therapy** | Limited | Better |

### Why Early MSA-P Can Be Misdiagnosed as PD
- Both present with bradykinesia, rigidity, and postural instability
- MSA-P may have a partial initial levodopa response (in up to 30–40% of patients)
- Autonomic symptoms may be subtle early on
- MRI may be normal in early MSA
- Tremor can occur in MSA-P

### When to Suspect MSA over PD
- **Rapid progression** — loss of independence within 1–3 years
- **Early prominent autonomic failure** — especially urinary symptoms or orthostatic hypotension within the first year
- **Poor or absent levodopa response** after adequate trial
- **Cerebellar features** — any ataxia is unusual for PD
- **Early falls** — especially within the first year
- **Inspiratory stridor** — essentially pathognomonic for MSA
- **Positive MRI findings** — hot cross bun sign, putaminal changes
- **Normal MIBG scan** in a patient with apparent parkinsonism

## Management

There is no cure or disease-modifying therapy for MSA. **All current management is symptomatic.** A multidisciplinary approach is essential, including neurology, urology, speech-language pathology, physical therapy, occupational therapy, respiratory medicine, and palliative care.

### Parkinsonism

| Agent | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Levodopa/carbidopa** | First-line; trial up to 1,000 mg/day levodopa; response partial and often decreases over time |
| **Amantadine** 100–400 mg/day | May provide modest benefit for motor symptoms and dyskinesias |
| **Dopamine agonists** | Limited evidence in MSA; use with caution (can worsen hypotension) |
| **MAO-B inhibitors** (selegiline, rasagiline) | Minimal benefit reported |
| **Anticholinergics** | If dystonia is prominent; use cautiously (urinary retention, cognitive effects) |

**Non-pharmacological:**
- Physical therapy: Gait training, balance exercises, fall prevention
- Occupational therapy: Adaptive equipment, home safety assessment
- Speech therapy: Dysarthria management, swallowing strategies, communication aids

### Neurogenic Orthostatic Hypotension (nOH)

**Non-pharmacological (first-line):**
- Increase fluid intake (2–2.5 L/day) and salt intake (>8 g/day)
- Head-up tilt of bed during sleep (30°)
- Compression stockings and abdominal binders
- Avoid prolonged standing, hot environments, large meals, alcohol
- Physical countermeasures: leg crossing, squatting, tensing leg muscles before standing

**Pharmacological:**
| Agent | Dose | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Midodrine** | 2.5–30 mg/day in 3 divided doses | Alpha-1 agonist; increases peripheral vascular resistance |
| **Fludrocortisone** | 0.05–0.2 mg/day | Mineralocorticoid; expands intravascular volume |
| **Droxidopa** | 100–600 mg 3×/day | Norepinephrine precursor |
| **Atomoxetine** | 10–36 mg/day | NE reuptake inhibitor |

**Caution:** Supine hypertension is common and complicates management — avoid treating at the expense of severe supine hypertension.

**Supine hypertension management:**
- Sleep with head of bed elevated 6–9 inches
- Short-acting antihypertensives at bedtime: transdermal nitroglycerin, losartan, nifedipine, hydralazine, or clonidine

### Urinary Dysfunction

| Problem | Agent | Dose |
|---|---|---|
| Urge incontinence | **Mirabegron** | 25–50 mg/day |
| | **Trospium** | 20–40 mg/day |
| | **Solifenacin** | 5–10 mg/day |
| Nocturia | **Desmopressin** | 10–40 mcg intranasally at bedtime (monitor for hyponatremia) |
| Incomplete emptying / retention | **Tamsulosin** | 0.4 mg/day |
| | **Prazosin** | 1–3 mg/day |
| | Clean intermittent catheterization | As needed; often ultimately required |

### Erectile Dysfunction

- PDE-5 inhibitors: Sildenafil 50 mg, Vardenafil 10 mg, Tadalafil 20 mg
- **Use with caution:** May worsen orthostatic hypotension

### Constipation

**Non-pharmacological:** Increase fluids, fiber, exercise; eliminate constipating drugs where possible

**Pharmacological:**
| Agent | Dose |
|---|---|
| Macrogol (PEG) | 13–39 g/day |
| Lactulose | 20–60 g/day |
| Sodium picosulfate | 5–10 mg/day |

### REM Sleep Behavior Disorder (RBD)

| Agent | Dose |
|---|---|
| **Clonazepam** | 0.5–2 mg at bedtime (first-line) |
| **Melatonin** | 2–5 mg at bedtime (first-line, preferred if comorbidities) |
| Cannabidiol | 50–300 mg (refractory cases) |
| Gabapentin | 300–800 mg (refractory cases) |
| Pregabalin | 25–100 mg (refractory cases) |

**Safety counseling:** Bed rails, mattress on floor, removal of sharp/heavy objects near bed

### Sleep-Disordered Breathing / Inspiratory Stridor

- CPAP or BiPAP for obstructive and central sleep apnea
- Nocturnal positive pressure ventilation for stridor
- **Tracheostomy** in advanced disease with severe, refractory stridor — requires careful goals-of-care discussion

### Cerebellar Ataxia

- No established pharmacological treatments
- Off-label options with limited evidence: propranolol, baclofen, amantadine, gabapentin, buspirone
- Physical and occupational therapy essential
- Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) — investigational

### Dysarthria

- Speech-language pathology: Voice amplification, augmentative/alternative communication (AAC) devices
- Lee Silverman Voice Treatment (LSVT) — limited evidence in MSA

### Dysphagia

- Swallowing evaluation: Modified barium swallow or FEES
- Dietary texture modifications (thickened liquids, soft foods)
- Aspiration precautions
- Percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) when oral intake is insufficient or aspiration risk is high — requires goals-of-care discussion

### Drooling (Sialorrhea)

- Anticholinergic medications (glycopyrrolate, atropine drops)
- Botulinum toxin A injections to parotid/submandibular glands (use caution — risk of worsening dysphagia)

### Psychological / Psychiatric Symptoms

- Depression/anxiety: SSRIs or SNRIs (modest benefit; evidence limited)
- Fluoxetine has shown some modest motor benefit in observational data
- Psychiatric referral, counseling, and support groups
- Caregiver support is equally critical

### Multidisciplinary Team Coordination

Optimal MSA care requires:
- **Neurology** (movement disorder specialist preferred)
- **Urology** or **Urogynecology**
- **Pulmonology / Sleep Medicine**
- **Speech-Language Pathology**
- **Physical and Occupational Therapy**
- **Dietitian**
- **Palliative Care**
- **Social Work**

## Emerging and Investigational Therapies

*Note: As of 2025–2026, no disease-modifying therapy has received regulatory approval for MSA.*

| Therapy | Mechanism | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Amlenetug** (PRX002/RG7935) | Anti-alpha-synuclein antibody | Phase II — showed slowing of progression in early-disease subgroup |
| **ATH434** (Alterity Therapeutics) | Iron chelator; reduces alpha-synuclein aggregation | Phase II (ATH434-202) — promising 12-month biomarker/clinical data (July 2025) |
| **PD01A / PD03A** | Alpha-synuclein peptide vaccines | Phase I — demonstrated safety |
| **Mesenchymal stem cells (intrathecal)** | Neuroprotection, immune modulation | Small trials — significantly lower progression rate vs. historical controls |
| **Epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG)** | Green tea polyphenol; anti-aggregation | Phase II — ineffective |
| **Sirolimus** (mTOR inhibitor) | Autophagy enhancement | Phase II — no clinical benefit |

**Clinical Trial Registry:** Patients and families should be referred to [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov) and [MSA Coalition clinical trials page](https://www.msacoalition.org) for current enrollment opportunities.

## Prognosis

MSA carries a **significantly worse prognosis than Parkinson's disease.**

| Milestone | Timeline from Symptom Onset |
|---|---|
| Time to diagnosis (probable) | ~2 years |
| Need for walking aid | ~3 years |
| Wheelchair dependence | ~5–6 years (50–60% of patients) |
| Bedridden | ~6–8 years (50% of patients) |
| Median survival | **6–10 years** (range: 2–15 years) |

**Causes of death:**
- Aspiration pneumonia (most common)
- Urosepsis
- Respiratory failure
- Sudden unexplained death in sleep (SUDEP-like events)
- Pulmonary embolism

**Prognostic factors:**
- Earlier age of onset associated with longer survival
- MSA-C may have slightly longer survival than MSA-P (debated)
- Inspiratory stridor is a poor prognostic sign
- Late-onset MSA (>70 years) may have slower progression in some cases (emerging evidence, 2025)
- Vitamin B12 deficiency in MSA patients has been associated with shorter survival

## Palliative and End-of-Life Care

Palliative care should be **introduced early**, ideally at or near the time of diagnosis — not reserved for end of life. MSA's rapid trajectory makes early goals-of-care conversations essential.

### Goals-of-Care Topics to Address

- Understanding of diagnosis and prognosis
- Preferences for life-sustaining interventions (PEG tube, tracheostomy, mechanical ventilation, CPR)
- Advance directives / POLST / living will
- Surrogate decision-maker designation (healthcare proxy / POA)
- Hospice eligibility and timing
- Brain donation (for research — see resources below)

### Hospice Criteria

MSA patients may qualify for hospice when expected survival is ≤6 months. **Hospice eligibility indicators:**
- Rapidly declining functional status
- Dysphagia with aspiration, refusal of PEG
- Recurrent pneumonias
- Weight loss >10% over 6 months
- Unable to ambulate, dress, or bathe without significant assistance
- Severe orthostatic hypotension unresponsive to treatment

### Common End-of-Life Concerns

- Dysphagia and aspiration pneumonia management
- Respiratory distress / stridor
- Pain (less prominent in MSA than some other terminal illnesses, but present)
- Anxiety and existential distress
- Caregiver burnout (MSA caregiving burden is enormous)
