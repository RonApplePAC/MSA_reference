# MSA: The Primary Care Provider's Role

> **Context:** Neurology is managing the MSA diagnosis and movement disorder. This document defines what the primary care PA-C or physician should own, monitor, and act on between neurology visits.

---

## Your Core Role

As the PCP, you are the **hub of continuity** — not the specialist managing the MSA itself, but the clinician who:

1. Monitors for complications between neurology visits
2. Manages comorbidities without worsening MSA symptoms
3. Coordinates referrals neurology may not initiate (urology, pulm/sleep, SLP, PT/OT, palliative care)
4. Recognizes and escalates deterioration
5. Manages medications that interact with MSA physiology
6. Leads advance care planning (this often falls to PCP)
7. Supports and screens the caregiver

MSA is rapidly progressive. A patient who was ambulatory 6 months ago may need a wheelchair evaluation now. Your visits are not routine — every encounter should include an active functional reassessment.

---

## At Every Visit: What to Check

### 1. Orthostatic Vital Signs — Every Time

This is non-negotiable. Measure BP/HR supine, then at 1 minute and 3 minutes standing.

- **Neurogenic orthostatic hypotension (nOH):** Drop ≥20 mmHg SBP or ≥10 mmHg DBP within 3 minutes of standing
- **Supine hypertension** (common and often coexists — complicates treatment)
- Document trends. Both conditions can worsen as disease progresses and with medication changes.
- Ask about symptoms: dizziness, lightheadedness, syncope, "coat hanger" neck/shoulder pain, near-falls upon standing

**What to do:**
- Reinforce non-pharmacological measures (fluids 2–2.5 L/day, salt >8 g/day, compression stockings, head-up tilt of bed, rise slowly, avoid large meals and alcohol)
- If orthostasis is symptomatic and untreated or undertreated, contact neurology — or manage with midodrine, fludrocortisone, or droxidopa per neurology's guidance
- If supine hypertension is new or worsening, flag to neurology

---

### 2. Functional Status — Every Visit

Ask specifically:
- Are they still walking independently? Using a cane, walker, wheelchair?
- Can they dress, bathe, prepare food without help?
- Have there been any falls? Injuries?
- Can they use a telephone / communicate their needs?

**Why this matters:** Functional decline is the clearest sign of disease progression. Document it. It also drives hospice eligibility conversations.

---

### 3. Urinary Symptoms

MSA causes significant urinary dysfunction — often more severe than PD. Ask:
- Urgency, frequency, urge incontinence?
- Difficulty initiating void or incomplete emptying?
- Current catheter use?
- Recurrent UTIs?

**What to do:**
- Perform a post-void residual (bladder scan or in-office catheter) if retention is suspected — residual >100 mL warrants urology referral
- Treat UTIs promptly — urosepsis is a cause of death in MSA
- Coordinate with urology if not already established — urodynamic studies may be indicated
- If clean intermittent catheterization (CIC) is needed, ensure patient/caregiver has been taught and has supplies

---

### 4. Respiratory / Sleep Symptoms

- Ask about **inspiratory stridor** — a high-pitched noise when breathing in during sleep (often reported by bed partner). This is a distinctive and serious MSA feature.
- Snoring, witnessed apnea, excessive daytime sleepiness, unrefreshing sleep
- Morning headaches (CO2 retention)

**What to do:**
- If stridor is new or worsening, escalate to neurology and refer to pulmonology/sleep medicine urgently — respiratory failure during sleep is a cause of sudden death in MSA
- If sleep apnea suspected: refer for polysomnography
- CPAP/BiPAP are used for sleep-disordered breathing; if not established, facilitate referral

---

### 5. Swallowing and Nutrition

- Ask about coughing or choking with eating or drinking, "wet" voice after meals, difficulty with thin liquids vs. solids
- Track weight — even 5–10% unintentional loss is significant
- Ask about aspiration events, pneumonia history

**What to do:**
- Refer to speech-language pathology (SLP) for formal swallow evaluation if not already done — or repeat if swallowing has changed
- If aspiration pneumonia has occurred, this is a major inflection point for goals-of-care discussion
- Consider dietary texture modifications (thickened liquids, soft/pureed diet)
- PEG tube: if oral intake is insufficient or aspiration risk is unacceptable — this requires a deliberate goals-of-care conversation before placement, not a reflexive referral

---

### 6. Depression, Anxiety, Cognitive Status

- Up to 40–50% of MSA patients have clinically significant depression or anxiety
- Rapid disease progression, loss of independence, and caregiver strain all compound this
- Mild cognitive impairment is common (~40%); frank dementia is rare early in MSA (if present, question diagnosis)

**What to do:**
- Screen with PHQ-9 and GAD-7 at regular intervals
- SSRIs are first-line for depression in MSA; fluoxetine has some observational support for modest motor benefit as well
- Refer to counseling or psychology if available
- If cognitive concern: formal neuropsychological testing helps distinguish MSA from DLB (dementia with Lewy bodies)

---

### 7. Caregiver Assessment

MSA caregiving is among the most demanding in neurology. Caregiver burnout is common and a patient safety issue — an exhausted caregiver makes errors, misses symptoms, and is at risk for depression themselves.

**Assess at every visit:**
- Who is the primary caregiver?
- How are they coping?
- Are they sleeping?
- Are they getting any breaks (respite care)?
- Do they know about MSA Coalition support resources?

**What to do:**
- Refer to social work for resource navigation
- Encourage caregiver to establish their own PCP care and attend their own appointments
- Discuss home health aide, adult day programs, and eventual memory care or skilled nursing planning early — before a crisis forces the decision

## Medications to Watch

Many common medications worsen MSA symptoms. Review the med list at every visit.

### Drugs That Worsen Orthostatic Hypotension

| Drug Class | Examples | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha-1 blockers | Tamsulosin, terazosin, prazosin | Significant OH worsening — use only when necessary for urinary retention, start low |
| Diuretics | HCTZ, furosemide | Volume depletion worsens OH |
| Nitrates | Isosorbide, nitroglycerin | Vasodilation worsens OH |
| PDE-5 inhibitors | Sildenafil, tadalafil | Can significantly worsen OH — use with caution |
| Tricyclic antidepressants | Amitriptyline, nortriptyline | Orthostatic effects; avoid if possible |
| Antipsychotics (typical) | Haloperidol | Worsen parkinsonism AND OH |
| Beta-blockers | Metoprolol, atenolol | Impair reflex tachycardia response to OH |
| Levodopa | Carbidopa/levodopa | Can worsen OH — neurology manages, but monitor vitals after dose changes |

### Drugs That Worsen Parkinsonism (Avoid if Possible)

| Drug Class | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Antipsychotics | Haloperidol, risperidone, olanzapine | Avoid; worsen parkinsonism significantly |
| Antiemetics | Metoclopramide, prochlorperazine | Dopamine blockade — avoid; use ondansetron instead |
| Antivertigo agents | Meclizine (chronic use) | Anticholinergic; use cautiously |

### Drugs That Can Cause Urinary Retention (Worsen Incomplete Emptying)

| Drug Class | Examples |
|---|---|
| Anticholinergics | Oxybutynin, tolterodine, diphenhydramine, tricyclics |
| Antihistamines | Benadryl (avoid in MSA) |
| Decongestants | Pseudoephedrine (also raises BP — caution with supine HTN) |

> **Rule of thumb:** Before adding any new medication in an MSA patient, ask: does this lower BP, block dopamine, or cause urinary retention? If yes, find an alternative or consult neurology first.

## Referrals You Should Own

Neurology manages the movement disorder and may initiate some of these, but many fall through the cracks without a PCP actively coordinating:

| Referral | Indication | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| **Urology** | Urinary retention, recurrent UTIs, incontinence | Early — don't wait for severe symptoms |
| **Sleep Medicine / Pulmonology** | Stridor, sleep apnea, excessive daytime sleepiness | Urgently if stridor present |
| **Speech-Language Pathology** | Dysarthria, dysphagia, voice changes | At diagnosis; repeat when symptoms change |
| **Physical Therapy** | Gait instability, falls, mobility decline | At diagnosis; periodically as function declines |
| **Occupational Therapy** | ADL limitations, fall prevention, adaptive equipment | Early; reassess with each functional decline |
| **Dietitian** | Weight loss, dysphagia dietary modifications | When swallowing or nutrition becomes an issue |
| **Social Work** | Caregiver support, resource navigation, home health setup | Early and ongoing |
| **Palliative Care** | Advance care planning, symptom burden, goals of care | Introduce at or soon after diagnosis |
| **Hospice** | When prognosis is ≤6 months or patient declines further intervention | See criteria below |

## When to Escalate to Neurology (Don't Wait for the Scheduled Visit)

Contact the neurology team if:

- **New or worsening inspiratory stridor** — serious respiratory risk
- **Rapid functional decline** over weeks (new falls, new wheelchair need, new dysphagia)
- **Syncope** or near-syncope despite current orthostatic hypotension management
- **Aspiration pneumonia** — may prompt PEG/goals-of-care discussion
- **New or worsening severe supine hypertension** (SBP >180 while supine)
- **Levodopa side effects** — hallucinations, dyskinesias, significant OH worsening
- **New cognitive change** — may indicate another diagnosis or intercurrent illness
- **Medication conflict** that neurology needs to weigh in on
- **Depression with suicidal ideation** — screen for this; it is documented in MSA

## Advance Care Planning — This Is Your Job

Neurologists are often focused on symptom management. The advance care planning conversation frequently does not happen unless the PCP initiates it. MSA's median survival is 6–10 years, with rapid functional decline. These conversations need to happen **early**, while the patient can meaningfully participate.

### Topics to Address (Stage by Stage)

**At or shortly after diagnosis:**
- Does the patient understand the diagnosis and its trajectory?
- Establish healthcare proxy / durable power of attorney for healthcare
- Document goals of care in the chart
- Introduce POLST/MOLST form if your state uses it

**As disease progresses (wheelchair-dependent stage):**
- Revisit feeding tube (PEG): patient's wishes before a crisis forces the decision
- Discuss mechanical ventilation and CPR preferences
- Introduce palliative care formally if not already engaged

**Advanced disease (bedridden, severe dysphagia, recurrent pneumonia):**
- Hospice eligibility discussion (see below)
- Brain donation: inform families this is possible and provides diagnostic confirmation; refer to Brain Support Network (brainsupportnetwork.org — free service)

### Hospice Eligibility Red Flags to Document

MSA patients may qualify for hospice when:
- Unable to ambulate without substantial assistance
- Unable to dress or bathe without assistance
- Dysphagia with aspiration risk and patient declining PEG
- Recurrent aspiration pneumonia
- Weight loss >10% over 6 months
- Severe, refractory orthostatic hypotension
- Rapid overall decline trajectory

## Comorbidity Management Considerations

MSA does not protect a patient from their other diseases. Manage comorbidities, but with MSA physiology in mind:

| Comorbidity | Special Consideration in MSA |
|---|---|
| **Hypertension** | Tricky — supine hypertension is common but aggressive treatment worsens orthostatic hypotension. Work with neurology. |
| **Diabetes** | Autonomic neuropathy is already present; hypoglycemia can be harder to detect. Monitor carefully. |
| **BPH** | Alpha-blockers worsen OH significantly. Consider urology for alternatives; CIC may be better than alpha-blockers. |
| **Depression** | Treat — SSRIs are preferred; avoid TCAs |
| **Osteoporosis** | Falls are frequent and early — bone density screening and fall prevention are critical |
| **Atrial fibrillation / anticoagulation** | Falls + anticoagulation = bleeding risk. Discuss with patient and consider fall risk carefully. |
| **Sleep disorders (other)** | Review sleep meds — benzodiazepines (except clonazepam for RBD), sedating antihistamines increase fall risk |

---

## Practical Communication Tips with Neurology

- **Share visit notes promptly.** Neurology needs to know about functional changes, new falls, new UTIs, orthostatic vital signs, and medication changes you've made.
- **Ask for a clear medication list** from neurology after each visit. Know what they're adjusting and why.
- **Call or message, don't just refer.** For acute concerns (new stridor, syncope, rapid decline), a direct call or message to the neurology team is faster and safer than a routine referral.
- **Coordinate timing:** Try not to schedule major medication changes from your side within days of a neurology appointment without communication — drug interactions across providers are a real risk in MSA given the polypharmacy required.

## Summary: Your PCP/PA-C Checklist

| Domain | Action |
|---|---|
| Orthostatic vitals | Measure every visit; trend over time |
| Functional status | Document ADLs, mobility, falls at every visit |
| Urinary symptoms | Screen, check PVR if retention suspected, refer urology |
| Sleep / respiratory | Ask about stridor and apnea; refer sleep medicine if indicated |
| Swallowing / nutrition | Track weight; refer SLP; plan PEG discussion early |
| Mood | PHQ-9 + GAD-7 regularly; treat depression |
| Caregiver | Assess burden and coping at every visit |
| Medications | Audit for BP-lowering, dopamine-blocking, and anticholinergic agents |
| Referrals | Own the coordination: urology, SLP, PT/OT, palliative care |
| Advance directives | Initiate and document; repeat as disease progresses |
| Escalation | Know when to contact neurology without waiting |
